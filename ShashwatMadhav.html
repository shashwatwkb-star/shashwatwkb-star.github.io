What drives me

⚛️
The quantum frontier

Quantum computing sits at the edge of what's physically computable. The idea that a qubit can hold superposition — be two things at once — and that entanglement can link particles across space: that's not abstraction, it's reality we can engineer. I want to be someone who does that engineering.

🔬
Physics as code

The Jaynes-Cummings model, photon-matter interactions, energy exchange in quantum circuits — these aren't just equations. They're programs waiting to be written. Computational physics is where I translate the language of the universe into something a machine can simulate. My Physics minor gives me the formal foundation — Lagrangian mechanics, statistical physics — to do it properly.

🧠
Intelligence from data

ML and deep learning let me find structure where there seems to be none — predicting churn, pricing houses, understanding sentiment in text. I'm drawn to the moment a model suddenly generalises: when the pattern becomes the prediction.

⚡
Building real systems

Working on Vega Racing Electric's EV systems taught me that hardware is unforgiving. Battery management, motor controllers, precision assembly — real-world engineering doesn't compile; it either works or it doesn't. That pressure sharpens everything.

🎹
Structure in everything

Piano gave me an obsession with structure. A chord progression is a decision tree. A Beethoven sonata is an algorithm with emotion as the loss function. I bring that same attention to architecture — in code, in circuits, in ideas.

🌐
Open collaboration

At PES Open Source I learned that the best solutions come from building in public, with others. Open-source isn't just a licence — it's a philosophy: share the work, share the knowledge, let the best ideas win.

What interests me

Quantum computing is not just a faster computer — it's a fundamentally different model of computation. I'm fascinated by how superposition and entanglement can be harnessed to solve problems that classical machines can't touch.

My hands-on work with the Jaynes-Cummings model showed me that quantum optics and quantum computing are deeply linked — the same gates that manipulate qubits also describe light-matter interactions in cavities.

Longer-term: I want to work on quantum algorithms for simulation, specifically in chemistry and materials science — areas where quantum advantage is most tangible.

Alongside my B.Tech in Electronics & Communications, I'm pursuing a minor in Physics at PES University. It's the formal backbone behind everything I simulate — the maths and mechanics underneath the circuits I build.

Current course: UE26PH251 — Classical and Statistical Physics. It runs from classical mechanics (constraints, D'Alembert's principle, Euler-Lagrange equations, Noether's theorem, central forces, Rutherford scattering) through tensors, into statistical mechanics, partition functions, ensembles, and quantum statistics.

This is exactly the toolkit that connects to quantum simulation: ensembles and partition functions are how you move from single particles to systems, and Lagrangian thinking is how you set up a problem before you ever write code.
